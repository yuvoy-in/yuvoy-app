Most people who dive in the Andamans have never dived before. Swaraj Dweep —
still called Havelock by nearly everyone — is where that usually happens.

This is what the day actually involves, so you can decide before you are
standing on a jetty at six in the morning.

## You do not need a certification

A **try-dive** — PADI call it Discover Scuba Diving, other agencies have their
own name for the same thing — is a supervised introduction. No course, no
certification, no prior experience. An instructor takes you down and stays with
you.

**Depth is capped, and the cap is the safety mechanism.** A confined-water or
pool session is limited to around **6 metres**. The optional open-water dive
that follows goes no deeper than **12 metres**. If somebody offers you a first
dive substantially deeper than that, that is a reason to ask questions rather
than a better deal.

## Do you need to be able to swim?

Not in the sense most people mean. You do not need a swimming qualification and
you do not need to be fast or graceful.

What you do need is **comfort in water and the ability to keep yourself at the
surface.** That is a real requirement, not a formality — the surface is where
you spend the minutes before and after the dive, and panic there is the actual
risk.

If you are genuinely uneasy in water, say so when you book rather than on the
boat. It changes how an instructor runs the dive; it does not usually mean no.

## The medical form is the part nobody expects

Before you get in, you fill in a **diver medical questionnaire**. It asks about
conditions that behave differently under pressure — heart, lungs, ears,
sinuses, epilepsy, diabetes, recent surgery, some medications, pregnancy.

Two things worth knowing:

1. **A "yes" is not a no.** It means you may need a doctor's sign-off before
   diving. Some answers are cleared on the spot; some are not.
2. **Getting the sign-off in Havelock is hard.** If any answer might be a yes,
   see a doctor at home and bring the signed form. Discovering this on the
   morning of the dive is how people lose the thing they came for.

The current form is published by the training agency and you can read it before
you travel. Do that.

## Questions worth asking before you book

Not because operators are untrustworthy — most are careful — but because the
answers tell you what kind of day you are buying.

- **How many people per instructor?** For a first dive this is the number that
  matters most.
- **Is the maximum depth 12 metres?** You want a straight answer.
- **What happens if the sea is up?** Reschedule, refund, or nothing — worth
  knowing before rather than after. See [when to
  visit](/guides/when-to-visit-the-andamans) for why this comes up so often.
- **Is there a briefing on land before we go?** There should be.
- **What do I need to bring, and what is provided?**

## The honest expectation

A try-dive is a short time in the water — often twenty to thirty minutes at
depth — after a briefing and a boat ride that take considerably longer. People
who expect a full day of diving are sometimes surprised; people who expect
twenty minutes that change how they think about the sea usually are not.

If it works, the next step is a certification course, and Havelock is a
reasonable place to do one.
